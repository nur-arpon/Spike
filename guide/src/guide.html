Chapter 1

Before you start

Read this chapter once, all the way through, before you touch a soldering iron. It lists the tools, the safety rules for the battery and the iron, and how every step in this guide is laid out.

1.1What you are building

Desk Buddy is a small robot dog about 17 cm wide. Each of its four legs has a hip servo and a knee servo, and each paw has a wheel servo, so it can walk slowly or roll. A tail servo wags. Its face is a 4.3 inch touch screen, its eyes are a camera in the muzzle, and it hears you through two microphones.

You will build it in this order: print the parts, check every bought part, build the wiring harness, test everything on the desk, assemble the body, lid, head, legs, tail and ears, and finally switch it on and calibrate it.

Plan on roughly two days of printing at the maker space, an evening for the harness, an evening for the bench test and a weekend for the assembly. Nothing here is hard, but almost everything is small, so work slowly and in good light.

1.3Safety

Soldering and fumes

The iron tip is about 350 degrees C. Always put it back in its stand, never on the bench. Work by an open window or with a small fan pulling the smoke away from your face, not towards it. Wash your hands after soldering and before you eat. Wear glasses when you trim wire ends, because they fly.

18650 cells

These cells hold a lot of energy. Never let anything metal touch both ends of a cell: no loose cells in a pocket or a bag with keys or coins. Never use a cell with a torn or dented wrapper. Check the + and - marks twice before a cell goes into the holder, every single time. Never mix the Samsung cells with the spare Tinker cells, and always change both cells together.

Use the fuse

The protection board only cuts off at about 20 A, which is enough to melt thin wire inside a plastic shell. The 7.5 A fuse is what protects the robot. Never bridge it, and never replace it with a bigger one.

Power off before you touch the wiring

Switch the robot off before you plug or unplug anything. For any work inside the tub, also take the cells out. Keep your fingers away from the legs while the robot is switched on: servos are strong enough to pinch.

Heat

The printed parts are PLA, which softens at about 55 degrees C. Never leave the robot in a car or in direct sun behind a window.

1.4How to read this guide

Every assembly step looks the same. Here is what each part of a step means.

A2  The step number and title. The letter is the section: A Body, B Lid, C Head, D Legs, E Tail, F Ears and G Close up.

The picture. The part you fit in this step has an orange outline. A bought part it holds, like a servo or a sensor board, has a blue outline. Everything already built is faded. Orange arrows show which way a part moves to go on.

You need. The parts and the screws for this step. Screws are colour-coded by length, the same way every time:

  • M2 x 4
  • M2 x 6
  • M2 x 8
  • horn screw

The text. Read it all before you pick anything up. Then do it in order.

Check tells you how to know the step went right. Common mistake is the thing people most often get wrong at that step.

Left, right, front and back

Left and right always mean the robot's own left and right, as if you were sitting on it facing forward. The front is the end with the head. So when you face the robot, its left side is on your right.

Part names

Printed parts are named by their file, for example Thigh_FrontLeft_Outer. Bought parts use the names in the Parts check chapter.

Colours

Printed parts come in three colours: cream, brown and black (the small caps and covers). Most pictures in this edition were rendered before the colours were chosen, so they show the parts in off-white and dark grey; the plainer views drawn straight from the print files already use the new colours. Print the off-white parts in cream and the dark grey parts in brown. The small caps and covers stay black.

Screws into plastic

All screws are M2 self-tapping: they cut their own thread in the printed hole. Drive each screw in once before the part goes on, then take it out (this is called pre-threading). Tighten until the head touches, then a quarter turn more, and stop. Over-tightening strips the thread.

Chapter 4

Wiring

You build the whole wiring harness on the desk first: the battery pack, the power switch and fuse, and the hub with its two 5 V modules. The Bench test in chapter 5 then runs on this real harness, and the Assembly chapter only has to plug it in.

This chapter comes before the bench test because every test needs the battery and the 5 V modules. Read the ten rules first. They come from the parts audit, and each one prevents a way of destroying a board.

4.1The ten wiring rules

Rule 1

Never use the screen board's BAT port. That port is for one 3.7 V cell, and the 7.4 V pack would destroy the board. Feed 5 V into port P1, using only its 5 V and GND wires. Check each wire against the printed labels with the multimeter first.

Rule 2

Sensors get 3.3 V only. Every sensor, and the logic pin (VCC) of each servo board, runs on 3.3 V. Only the green V+ screw terminal of the servo boards gets 5 V. Before you connect any module, measure its SDA and SCL pins: they must read 3.3 V or less.

Rule 3

Build the battery pack in this order. Take the cells out of the holder. Solder a short wire to the metal link between the two cell positions: this is the middle wire the protection board needs. Check that the two cells are within 0.05 V of each other. Connect negative first, then the middle wire, then positive. Plug the charger in once to wake the protection board up.

Rule 4

The fuse comes straight after the protection board. It sits before both the charger and the switch, so a short anywhere in the robot blows the fuse instead of cooking a wire.

Rule 5

Charge with the robot switched off. Use a 5 V, 2 A phone charger and a USB-A to USB-C cable. This charger cannot run the robot while it charges.

Rule 6

Unplug the 5 V leads before you plug in the laptop. Before you connect a USB cable to the screen board or the camera board, unplug that board's 5 V lead from the hub. Two power sources on one board can damage it or the laptop port.

Rule 7

Wheel stop. The servo boards' OE pin goes to IO14, with the 2.2 kOhm resistor to 3.3 V. Do not use 10 kOhm, and never use IO46. The firmware turns on the watchdog, which restarts the board if the code freezes; while it restarts, OE is pulled high and every wheel stops. Run the first tests with the robot propped on a block.

Rule 8

Battery limits in the code. The firmware warns at 7.0 V, and at 6.8 V it parks the robot and switches the servos off.

Rule 9

Speaker. It plugs into the "Speak" port. Never connect either speaker wire to ground.

Rule 10

Servo setup. Centre each leg servo before you fit its horn. Each wheel servo's stop point is found in software.

4.2The power path

Power flows one way. The two cells in series make about 7.4 V. The protection board stops over-charge, over-drain and shorts, and the 7.5 A fuse comes straight after it, before both the charger and the switch. The switch feeds the hub, where two Mini560 modules each make 5 V: module #1 only for the servos, module #2 only for the screen and camera boards. They are kept apart so the servos' current spikes cannot reset the brain. A 1000 uF capacitor across each output smooths the spikes. A 10 k and 4.7 k resistor pair lets the screen board read the battery level on IO5.

The charger connects after the fuse and before the switch, so it charges the cells whether the robot is on or off. Switch the robot off to charge.

4.3Build the battery pack and power leads

Cells out

Do all of this with the cells out of the holder. They go in for the first time in the bench test (5.3), and come out again before you assemble the robot.

1. Middle wire. Inside the cell holder, find the metal link that joins the two cell positions. Put a touch of flux on it, and solder on a 10 cm thin wire. Be quick: the holder's plastic melts. This wire goes to the protection board's BM (middle) pad.

2. Holder to protection board. Shorten the holder's red and black leads to about 12 cm: long enough for the battery door to come off and sit beside the robot while it is still wired. Solder black to B-, the middle wire to BM, and red to B+.

3. Fuse. Solder one lead of the fuse holder to P+. Cut two thick red wires: 8 cm for the charger and 12 cm for the switch. Twist both onto the fuse holder's other lead, solder the joint, and cover it with heat-shrink.

4. Charger. Solder the 8 cm red wire to the charger's BAT+ (sometimes marked OUT+). Solder an 8 cm thick black wire from P- to the charger's BAT-.

5. Switch. Solder the 12 cm red wire to one switch terminal and a 25 cm thick red wire to the other; that one goes to the hub's BATT-SW pad. Solder a 25 cm thick black wire to P- as well; it goes to the hub's GND.

6. Finish. Slide heat-shrink over every joint before you solder it, then shrink it. Flag every loose end with tape: CHG+, CHG-, SW, HUB+, HUB-.

Check

With the multimeter on beeper mode, P+ to P- must NOT beep, and CHG+ to CHG- must NOT beep. The two switch wires beep only when the switch is ON.

4.4The signals

The screen board is the brain. One I2C bus, on its port P4 (IO17 for data, IO18 for clock, plus 3.3 V and GND), goes down through the collar to the hub, and from there a short lead of four wires goes to every sensor and both servo boards. Each device has its own address, shown in the diagram. The seven lasers all start at the same address, 0x29; at start-up the firmware wakes them one at a time with their XSHUT wires (from servo board B) and gives each a new address.

IO14 is the wheel stop. It drives the OE pin of both servo boards, and a 2.2 kOhm resistor pulls it up to 3.3 V. While the brain runs, it holds IO14 low and the servos work. If the brain crashes or restarts, the resistor pulls OE high and every servo output stops, so the wheels stop.

The two microphones share three wires from port P3. The speaker plugs into the "Speak" port. The camera board only needs 5 V and GND: it talks to the laptop over Wi-Fi.

4.5Build the hub

1. Board. Cut a perfboard to 50 x 35 mm: score both sides along a row of holes with a knife and snap it over the edge of the table.

2. Modules. Place the two Mini560 modules side by side, pads up. Solder the thick BATT-SW red wire to module #1's IN+ and run a short thick red link on to module #2's IN+. Do the same with the thick GND black wire to both IN- pads. The perfboard only holds the modules; the current runs in the thick wires.

3. Capacitors. Lay each 1000 uF capacitor on its side, and solder it across one module's OUT+ and OUT-. The stripe on the capacitor marks its minus leg; it goes to OUT-.

4. Servo power. From module #1's output, solder a 20 cm thick red and black pair for each servo board's green terminal. From module #2's output, make a red and black lead for the screen board (on an MX1.25 plug: use only its 5 V and GND wires, and cut and insulate the other two) and a Dupont red and black pair for the camera board.

5. Resistors. Solder the 10 k from BATT-SW to a spare pad (VBAT), and the 4.7 k from VBAT to GND; add the white wire from VBAT, for IO5. Solder the 2.2 k from another pad (OE) to the 3V3 rail, and the green wires from OE: one to IO14, one to each servo board.

6. Sensor rails. Run four bare wires along the rest of the board as rails: 3V3, GND, SDA and SCL. Solder a row of four header pins across the rails for each device (use the loose header strips that came with the sensor boards). The input from the screen board's P4 lead lands on one of these rows.

Check before it ever sees power

Beeper mode: BATT-SW to GND must not beep. Each module's OUT+ to OUT- must not beep. The 3V3 rail to GND must not beep. SDA to SCL must not beep.

4.6Servo boards: address and leads

Board B's address. Each servo board has six small solder pads marked A0 to A5. On the board that will go on the robot's right wall, bridge the A0 pad with a blob of solder across its two halves. That board is now "board B", address 0x41. Leave board A (left wall) as it came, address 0x40. Mark both boards with tape.

Leads. On the six-pin header at the side of each board, connect GND (black), OE (green), SCL (yellow), SDA (blue) and VCC (orange, 3.3 V). Leave the header's V+ pin empty: servo power goes only on the green screw terminal, V+ and GND.

VCC is 3.3 V

VCC is the chip's own supply, 3.3 V from the hub rail. Putting 5 V on VCC makes the board's data pins 5 V and can damage the screen board.

Bench test

Test every servo, board and sensor on the desk before any of it goes inside the robot. A dead sensor found now costs five minutes. The same sensor found after assembly means taking the lid, the side panels and a leg off again.

5.1Get ready

Header pins. Solder the header pins onto every sensor board: the eight laser boards, the motion sensor, the touch board, the gesture sensor and the two microphones. Push the pins into the breadboard to hold them straight, lay the sensor on top, and solder. This is good practice before the harder joints.

Software. The servo test sketch is in guide/tools/servo_center. PlatformIO is already installed in the laptop project's Python environment. Every command below is typed in a terminal (PowerShell) in that folder:

cd D:\Claude-Projects\RobotCompanion\guide\tools\servo_center
$pio = "D:\Claude-Projects\RobotCompanion\software\laptop\.venv\Scripts\pio.exe"
& $pio run                  # build (checked: builds cleanly, 28 Sep 2026)
& $pio run -t upload        # upload to the screen board
& $pio device monitor       # open the Serial Monitor (Ctrl+C to leave)

Plug the screen board into the laptop with the USB-C cable, and nothing else. It should light up and run the seller's demo. Touch the screen: the demo should react. That proves the screen, the touch chip and the board.

A servo's horn must go on while the servo sits at its centre, 90 degrees, or the leg will stand crooked. The test sketch does this for all thirteen servos at once, and keeps them there.

Range check and joint limits

You need

  • all four legs fitted

Before the lid goes on, move every joint through its whole range by hand, slowly: each hip about 30 degrees forward and back, each knee about 50 degrees both ways. Watch the leads in each hip cup and at each knee. Nothing may pinch, rub or pull tight.

Angles count from the straight-down pose. Plus moves the foot forward, toward the head, and minus moves it back. The hip angle turns the thigh; the knee angle turns the shin relative to the thigh. The left and right legs are mirror images, so the firmware maps these angles to each servo's own direction.

Front legs need no limit inside hip plus or minus 30 and knee plus or minus 50 degrees. Back legs: when the hip is above plus 25 degrees, keep the knee at or below plus 45 degrees. At hip plus 30 and knee plus 50 the back paw would come within 0.9 mm of the belly; with this rule the gap never drops below 4.6 mm. Walking never gets near these poses. They only matter for tricks like sitting or folding a leg.

Checks before the cells go in

  • multimeter
  • a block or mug to prop the robot on

7.2

Cells in, protection board awake

You need

  • 2 x Samsung 35E cells
  • USB-C charger and cable

7.5

If something does not move

Switch off first, every time, before you touch a plug.

One servo limp: its plug is on the wrong channel, on the wrong board, or upside down. Check the servo channel table; the brown wire goes on the GND row.

Every servo limp: the servo boards are not getting 5 V, or the wheel-stop line is high. Measure between V+ and GND on each board's green terminal: it must read about 5 V. Then check the green OE wire goes to IO14 and the 2.2 kOhm resistor goes to 3.3 V, not to 5 V. Type c in the Serial Monitor to switch the outputs on again.

A servo board "NOT FOUND": check its VCC reads 3.3 V, its SDA and SCL go to the right rails, and, for board B, that the A0 pad is bridged.

A wheel keeps turning: it is normal for a 360 degree servo to creep at 1500 us. Find its real stop point in the next step.

7.6

You need

  • the calibration table in this step
  • a pencil

In the Serial Monitor press n until the servo you want is selected (it wiggles). Press + or - to move it 10 us at a time until the leg is exactly straight, or the tail exactly straight back. Write the number in the table.

For each wheel, press + or - until the wheel stops completely, and write that number down as its stop point.

Finally, for each hip and knee, check which way plus moves it. Plus must move the foot forward, toward the head. If it moves back, write "reversed" in the table. The left and right legs are usually opposite.

Before long walks, check how hot the knee pods get with the multimeter's thermocouple, if it has one: stop and let the robot cool above 45 degrees C.

Chapter 8

Troubleshooting

Find the symptom, check the likely cause, then do the fix. Switch the robot off before you touch any wire, every time.

Nothing happens when I switch on.
Likely cause: The protection board is asleep, the fuse has blown, or a cell is in the wrong way round.
Fix: Switch off. Measure P+ to P- at the protection board: 0 V means it is asleep, so plug the USB-C charger in for a few seconds. If it still reads 0 V, check the fuse and the cells' + and - marks.
The fuse blows as soon as I switch on.
Likely cause: A short circuit after the fuse: a bare wire end, a solder blob, or a screw tip touching a board.
Fix: Take the fuse out. With the multimeter on beeper mode, test the switched battery wire to GND, then each 5 V module output to GND. Look for the joint that beeps and fix it before you fit a new fuse.
The screen board restarts when the legs move.
Likely cause: The servos pull the 5 V line down. Either the two 5 V modules are sharing a job, or a capacitor is missing.
Fix: Check that module #1 feeds only the servo boards and module #2 only the screen and camera boards, and that each has its 1000 uF capacitor, stripe to minus.
The screen board restarts when the lasers start up.
Likely cause: Seven lasers plus the other sensors ask too much of the screen board's 3.3 V supply.
Fix: Measure the 3.3 V rail while it happens. If it dips below 3.1 V, move the lasers' VIN to the 5 V boards rail, but only after checking that each laser's SDA and SCL pins still read 3.3 V or less. Otherwise add a separate 3.3 V module.
One servo does nothing.
Likely cause: Its plug is on the wrong channel or board, or upside down.
Fix: Check the servo channel table. The brown wire goes on the GND row. Use the servo sketch: press n until that servo is selected and watch whether it wiggles.
Every servo is limp.
Likely cause: No 5 V on the servo boards' green terminal, or the wheel-stop (OE) line is high.
Fix: Measure V+ to GND on each green terminal: about 5 V. Then check the green OE wire goes to IO14 and the 2.2 kOhm resistor goes to 3.3 V. In the servo sketch, press c.
A servo board is "NOT FOUND".
Likely cause: No 3.3 V on its VCC pin, SDA and SCL swapped or loose, or (board B) the A0 pad is not bridged.
Fix: Measure 3.3 V on VCC. Re-seat the SDA and SCL wires. Board B must show 0x41 in the scan; if both boards show as 0x40, bridge A0 on board B.
A wheel keeps creeping when it should stop.
Likely cause: Every 360 degree servo has its own stop point, a little away from 1500 us.
Fix: Find the stop point with + and - in the servo sketch and write it in the calibration table. The firmware uses that number.
A leg is not straight when its servo is centred.
Likely cause: The horn went onto the spline one tooth off, or the servo was turned by hand after centring.
Fix: Small errors are fixed with the trim in the calibration table. If the error is bigger than about half a tooth, take the horn screw out, lift the horn off, and refit it one tooth over.
A servo buzzes or gets warm while standing still.
Likely cause: It is pushing against a stop: a lead is pinched, a part rubs, or its trim asks for a pose it cannot reach.
Fix: Switch off, move the joint by hand to find what catches, and re-lay any lead. Check the joint limits for the back legs.
A laser reads a short distance all the time.
Likely cause: The protective film is still on the chip, or the chip is not centred in its window.
Fix: Peel the film off. Re-seat the board so the chip sits in the middle of the window, and keep the window clean.
Only one laser answers, or none.
Likely cause: All seven lasers start at the same address. The firmware wakes them one by one using the XSHUT wires.
Fix: Run l in the servo sketch. A laser that shows NO ANSWER has a loose XSHUT, SDA, SCL, VIN or GND wire, or its XSHUT is on the wrong channel of board B.
Touch does not register.
Likely cause: A foil pad is not clamped to its wire, or the MPR121 is not seen on the bus.
Fix: With the power off, check each pad wire beeps to its foil. Check 0x5A shows in the I2C scan.
The microphones hear nothing, or left and right are swapped.
Likely cause: Glue over the sound holes, a wrong pin on P3, or both mics with the same L/R setting.
Fix: Check the sound holes are open. One mic's L/R pin goes to GND and the other's to 3.3 V. Check the three data lines against the signal table.
No sound from the speaker.
Likely cause: The speaker is not in the "Speak" port, or a speaker wire was connected to ground.
Fix: Plug it into "Speak" only. Never connect either speaker wire to GND.
The laptop cannot find the board, or the upload fails.
Likely cause: A charge-only USB cable, or the board is also powered through P1.
Fix: Unplug the 5 V lead from P1 first. Try the phone cable test from the parts check: if the phone does not show up on the laptop, the cable is charge-only. Hold BOOT while you plug the cable in to force upload mode.
The Serial Monitor stays empty after an upload.
Likely cause: The screen board's USB-C goes to the chip's own USB port, not to a USB-serial chip.
Fix: In tools/servo_center/platformio.ini change ARDUINO_USB_CDC_ON_BOOT=0 to 1, upload again, and reopen the monitor.
A cover or panel will not snap shut.
Likely cause: A lead is trapped under its edge, or a print blob sits in the hook window.
Fix: Never force it. Open it, re-lay the lead in its channel, and clean the hook windows with the flush cutters.
A screw spins without tightening.
Likely cause: The pilot hole is stripped.
Fix: Take the screw out, put a drop of superglue in the hole, let it set for ten minutes, and drive the screw in gently. A heat-set insert is the long-term fix for covers you open often.
The knee pods get hot.
Likely cause: The knees work hard when the robot stands for a long time, and the pods are closed plastic.
Fix: Check with a thermocouple. Stop and let the robot cool above 45 degrees C. Let it sit or lie down between walks.
The robot tips when it lifts a foot.
Likely cause: The weight is not where the design expects: a heavy item moved, or the cells are different.
Fix: Check the hub and the battery sit in their fences. The firmware shifts the body about 8 mm before lifting a front foot; do not lift a foot without that shift.
The battery runs down fast.
Likely cause: Old or mismatched cells, or the servos hold a pose against a stop.
Fix: Charge both cells fully, check they are within 0.05 V, and look for a buzzing servo. Use the spare pair to compare.
